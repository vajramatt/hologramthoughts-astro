On Saturdays, Clementine and Beau walked.

Not the polite, pavement kind of walking that happened on school mornings, when her mother held her hand at crossings and Beau trotted on his lead and everyone was sensible. Saturday walking was different. Saturday walking was the kind that got mud on your knees and burrs in your socks and made your mother say, “Where on *earth* have you been?”

The Finch house sat at the edge of a village that sat at the edge of a valley that sat at the edge of something older, though no one talked about the older thing because the village had a post office and a church and a pub called The Green Man, and villages with post offices and churches and pubs do not generally go in for admitting they are on the edge of something older.

But they were. And Beau knew it. And on Saturdays, he and Clementine went looking for it.

They had a route. It was not a sensible route. It went down the lane past the Hendersons’ farm, where Mrs. Henderson’s goat stood on top of the oil tank and stared at everyone who passed with the absolute confidence of a creature who has claimed the highest ground and intends to keep it. It went through the stile at the bottom of Cotter’s Field, where the grass was long enough to swallow Clementine to her waist and Beau to his ears. It went along the stream that ran beneath the old stone bridge and then up, up through the birch wood to the top of the ridge where you could see the whole valley laid out below like a green and rumpled quilt.

This was Clementine’s favourite place. She called it the Watching Spot, because from here you could watch everything… the smoke from the village chimneys, the sheep on the far hills moving in their slow, woolly way, the shadow of clouds sliding across the fields like enormous grey animals grazing on the light.

Beau had a different name for it. But Beau had a different name for most things.

On this particular Saturday, the air was cold and bright and the sky was the kind of blue that looks like it has been scrubbed. Clementine wore her red wellies and her father’s old scarf, which was far too long for her and trailed behind her like a knitted tail. Beau wore nothing but himself, which was all he had ever needed.

They went down the lane. The goat stared. Clementine waved at the goat. The goat did not wave back but did shift slightly on the oil tank in a way that suggested it had registered the greeting and filed it for future consideration.

They went through Cotter’s Field. The long grass whispered against Clementine’s legs, and she pretended she was an explorer pushing through the jungle, which was a game that required her to narrate the expedition aloud.

“The brave explorer and her faithful hound press onward through the uncharted wilderness,” she announced, in a voice she had borrowed from a nature documentary. “They are searching for the legendary… the legendary…” She paused. “Beau, what are we searching for?”

Beau was not listening. He had stopped at the edge of the field, where the grass met the treeline and the stream began its quiet muttering over stones. His ears were forward. His nose was working.

But this was not the Mumpkins attention. This was not the tight, urgent alertness that came before the performance. This was something softer. Something almost respectful. He was standing the way you stand when you have come to the edge of someone else’s place and are waiting to be noticed.

“Beau?” Clementine waded through the grass to his side. “What is it?”

She looked where he was looking. The treeline. The stream. The old stone bridge with moss on its shoulders and ferns growing from the cracks between the stones.

There was nothing there. Obviously. There was never anything there.

But the air above the stream was doing something. She could not have described it to a grown-up, because grown-ups needed words to be sensible and what the air was doing was not sensible. It was… shimmering. Not like heat-shimmer on a road. More like the air itself was breathing. As though the space between the water and the bridge was alive in a way that the rest of the air was not.

Beau wagged his tail. Once. Slowly. The way you nod to someone you have not seen in a long time but have not forgotten.

And then it was over. The air stopped doing the thing. The stream went back to being a stream. The bridge went back to being a bridge. And Beau trotted forward with his tail up and his ears easy, and Clementine followed, and they crossed the bridge and went into the birch wood.

But she looked back. Just once. And she could have sworn… she could have *sworn*… that the ferns on the bridge had moved. Not in the wind. There was no wind. They had moved the way a curtain moves when someone who was standing behind it steps away.

The birch wood was silver and white and full of the kind of light that only birch woods know how to make… a light that comes from everywhere and nowhere and makes every ordinary thing look like it is being shown to you for the first time. Clementine loved the birch wood. She loved the way the trunks peeled and the way the leaves shivered and the way the ground was soft with years and years of fallen things becoming soil.

Beau moved differently in the birch wood. In the house, he was either relaxed or alert… Tumpkins or pre-Mumpkins, on or off, the guardian resting or the guardian roused. But in the wood, he was something else. He moved like he belonged. Like the wood was a room he had been in before and knew where the furniture was.

He did not chase the squirrels. This was unusual because Beau’s opinion on squirrels was strong and well-documented. But in the birch wood, he let them be. He walked past the rabbits at the edge of the clearing without a glance. He stepped around the places where the tree roots crossed the path as though he knew exactly which roots to step on and which to leave alone.

Clementine watched him. She was getting better at watching.

At the top of the ridge, they sat. Clementine sat on the flat rock that was her throne. Beau sat beside her, his shoulder against her knee, his eyes on the valley.

“Beau,” she said. “What do you see when you look at things?”

He turned his head and looked at her. His brown eyes. The one good ear up, the other flopped. The particular expression of a dog who is listening with his whole self.

“Because I think you see more than us,” she said. “I think you see a lot more than us.”

He put his chin on her knee. She scratched behind the floppy ear. The valley spread below them, green and gold, and the village sent up its small smokes, and somewhere a church bell rang twice for no particular reason, and the world was very large and very old and very beautiful.

They sat there for a long time.

On the way home, they stopped at the Grandmother’s house.

The Grandmother lived in a cottage at the other end of the village, a cottage with a blue door and a garden full of things growing in ways that suggested the Grandmother had made suggestions to the plants and the plants had mostly listened. She was Clementine’s mother’s mother, and she was small and sharp and had opinions about everything, and her opinions were usually right, which was the most annoying thing about her, according to Clementine’s mother.

She was in the kitchen when they arrived, making something in a pot that smelled like warmth itself.

“Clementine,” she said. “Boots off. And don’t tell me where you’ve been because your knees already have.”

Clementine kicked off her wellies. Beau walked in behind her and sat down on the kitchen floor, and the Grandmother looked at him.

“Beau,” she said.

Just that. His name. Not Tumpkins, not Mumpkins, not Rumpkins. Just Beau. She said it the way you say a word in a language you learned a long time ago and have never quite forgotten… with a care and a precision that suggested the word meant more than its sound.

Beau sat perfectly still. He did not wag. He did not pant. He looked at the Grandmother and the Grandmother looked at him, and something passed between them that Clementine could not read but could feel, the way you feel the bass note of a song in your chest before you hear it in your ears.

“Good boy,” said the Grandmother. And then she turned back to her pot and said, “Soup in ten minutes. Wash your hands.”

On the walk home, in the early dark with the streetlamps just flickering on, Clementine said, “Beau, why are you different with Gran?”

Beau trotted beside her, his breath making small clouds in the cold air.

“Everyone else calls you your silly names,” she said. “And you let them. But when Gran says your name, you go all…” She searched for the word. “Still. Like you’re standing to attention.”

Beau’s tail swayed gently. The streetlamp light caught his golden coat and for a moment, just a moment, he looked like something from a much older story than this one.

“I think Gran knows something,” said Clementine. “I think she knows something about you that we don’t.”

They walked home in the dark, and the village was quiet, and the stars were coming out, and Beau kept himself between Clementine and the shadows the whole way.

Every single shadow. Every single step.

*The outside-place.*

*The bright-child wants to walk. Good. The house is heavy with sameness and her fire burns low when the walls are too close. She needs the wind and the open-sky and the old-ground under her feet.*

*The lane is safe. The goat-on-the-high-thing watches everything and tells me with its eyes what has passed. Nothing today. The lane is clear.*

*Cotter’s long-grass. The bright-child plays her mouth-game… narrating, the mother calls it. I listen with one ear. The other ear is for the treeline.*

*The bridge.*

*I stop. The bright-child stops too. She is learning to read me. This is dangerous and necessary. Dangerous because the reading will lead her closer to the seeing. Necessary because the seeing is coming whether I teach her or not, and it is better to come to it gently.*

*The bridge-keeper is here. Old. Older than me. A water-guardian, moss-covered, slow as stone. We have met before. Not at this bridge. At a ford, once, in the time of the hill-people, when the rivers were wider and the crossings were fewer and every ford had a keeper.*

*It sees me. I see it. We do not speak… we have no shared mouth-sounds… but we know each other the way two sentries know each other when their watch-posts are within sight. A nod. A recognition. We are not the same kind, but we do the same work.*

*The bright-child feels it. The shimmer. She cannot see the keeper but she can feel the aliveness of the place where it stands. Good. Her noticing is growing. The fire is learning to look outward.*

*In the birch-wood, I walk the old paths. The wood remembers me. The roots know where my feet fall. The small-lives… the tree-runners, the ground-diggers, the winged-ones… are not mine to chase here. They are residents. I am a visitor. There are courtesies.*

*At the high-place, the bright-child asks me what I see.*

*I see the valley as it is now and as it was before the house-shapes came. I see the lines in the land where the old paths run, the ones the road-makers built over but did not erase. I see the between-places… some thin, some thick, some sleeping, some watchful. I see the village with its small fires burning in every house and the way those fires make a pattern from above, a constellation of warmth that the cold-smell must navigate around.*

*I see the bright-child’s fire, close and warm against my shoulder. Brighter than any fire in the valley. So bright the winged-things in the high branches turn their heads toward her the way flowers turn toward the sun.*

*She does not know. She will.*

*The old-mother’s house.*

*The old-mother says the name. The True-name. Beau. And inside the human sound I hear the old sound… Bealú… rolling underneath like a river under ice. She knows the old sound. I do not know how. Perhaps she was taught. Perhaps she remembers from a life I was not part of. Perhaps she simply has the hearing… the old hearing that most of them lose when they get their growing-teeth and start believing only in things they can put in their pockets.*

*She looks at me and I feel seen. Not the way the bright-child sees me, which is a seeing that is growing toward the truth. The old-mother’s seeing is already there. She knows. She has always known. She says Beau and means Bealú and I hear the old name ring like a bell inside its housing and I sit still because the True-name requires stillness the way the Mump-name requires motion and the Rump-name requires fury.*

*Good boy, she says.*

*Yes. I am. In every leg. In every fur. In every life.*

*Good boy.*

*The walk-home in the dark. The bright-child talks. I listen. I keep between her and the shadow-places. She does not notice this. She will.*

*The stars are coming through. The old-lights. They were here before the cold-smell. They will be here after. They do not guard the way I guard… they are too far, too bright, too much themselves. But they watch. And the watching matters. A watched darkness is not the same as an unwatched one.*

*We are almost home. The house-light is on. The mother is in the window. The father is in the door.*

*The corners are clear.*

*For now.*
