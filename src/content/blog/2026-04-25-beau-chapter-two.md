It happened on a Thursday.

Thursdays were ordinary days in the Finch house. Clementine came home from school and dropped her bag by the door and told Beau everything that had happened, which took a while because quite a lot had happened, including a boy named Oliver putting a raisin up his nose and having to go to the nurse.

Beau listened to all of it. He was an excellent listener. He put his chin on her knee and looked at her with his brown eyes and did not interrupt once, which was more than could be said for most people.

Her mother made tea. Her father came home and kissed the top of Clementine’s head and said, “How was school?” and Clementine said, “Oliver put a raisin up his nose,” and her father said, “Left or right?” which was exactly the right question, and Clementine loved him enormously for asking it.

The afternoon turned gold.

It happened slowly, the way it always did… the light thickening, the shadows stretching, the house settling into that strange soft hour between day and evening when everything looks like a painting of itself. Clementine’s mother called it “the magic hour.” Clementine’s father called it “the hour when I can’t find my glasses because the light is doing that thing again.”

Beau called it something else. But we are not ready for that yet.

He was lying by the fire. Full Tumpkins. Chin on paws. Sighing. The kind of dog you would paint if you were painting a picture called *Contentment* or *A Very Good Boy Indeed.*

Then his head came up.

Not fast. Not alarmed. Just… attentive. The way a person turns toward a sound they are not sure they heard. His ears went forward… even the floppy one, which managed a sort of hopeful lean. His nose twitched. Once. Twice.

Clementine saw it. She was sitting on the rug with a book about whales, and she saw the whole thing.

His eyes moved to the corner. The corner by the hallway, where the coats hung on their hooks and the umbrella stand held three umbrellas and a walking stick that belonged to no one and had been there since before the Finches moved in.

There was nothing in the corner.

But Beau was looking at it the way you look at a door you expect to open.

And then he was on his feet, and the shoe was in his mouth… her father’s left slipper, the brown one with the hole in the toe… and the performance had begun.

He tore through the kitchen like a furry cannonball. He skidded on the tiles and crashed into a chair. He leapt over the cat, who had not signed up for any of this and expressed her opinion loudly. He doubled back through the sitting room with the slipper flapping like a trophy and his eyes bright with what looked for all the world like pure, stupid, glorious joy.

“Mumpkins!” shouted her father, already up and giving chase. “You absolute menace!”

Her mother stood at the kitchen counter with a cup of tea held high above the chaos, the expression on her face suggesting she was rethinking several life decisions.

Clementine laughed. She couldn’t help it. She laughed until her sides ached and her eyes watered, because her father had just crashed into the same chair Beau had crashed into, and the chair had fallen over, and the cat had fled to the top of the bookshelf and was staring down at all of them with the particular fury of a creature who believes it deserves better.

The whole house was laughing. Even her mother, who was trying very hard not to.

And Beau dropped the slipper. Just like that. Mid-chase. He dropped it and sat down and panted, tongue out, tail wagging, and looked at them all as if to say, *Well. That was fun.*

Her father picked up the slipper. “You,” he said, pointing at Beau, “are a scoundrel.”

Beau wagged harder.

Her mother sipped her tea. “Mumpkins,” she said, but she was smiling.

Clementine stopped laughing.

Not because it wasn’t funny. It was. But because she had looked back at the corner by the hallway, the one Beau had been watching before the performance began.

The coats hung on their hooks. The umbrellas stood in their stand. The walking stick leaned where it always leaned.

But the air there had changed. She was sure of it. Before Beau’s performance, the corner had felt… thin. That was still the only word she had for it. Thin, and watching. And now it felt like a corner again. Just a corner. With coats and umbrellas and a walking stick that belonged to no one.

She looked at Beau. Beau looked at her.

And for just a moment, before he put his chin back on his paws and became Mr. Tumpkins again, she thought she saw something in his brown eyes that was not silly at all.

------

*The gold-hour.*

*Walls thinning. The between-place opening its mouth in the coat-corner. The old cold-smell, faint but there… pressing its face against the thin-place the way a child presses its nose to a window.*

*Watching the bright-child.*

*Her fire is big today. She was laughing about something… a boy, a small dried fruit, a nose… and the laughing made her sparks fly high and settle on the lampshade and the rug and the sleeping cat like small golden moths that only I can see.*

*The cold-smell wants the moths.*

*I know this wanting. It is old. Older than this house, older than the wood that made this house, older than the tree that made the wood. I have stood against this wanting in different legs and different fur on hills that are now under roads and beside rivers that have changed their names.*

*It will not have the moths. It will not have the singing. It will not have the fire.*

*I take the foot-thing. The soft one with the hole. The father loves this foot-thing and will chase me for it and the chasing makes the loud-laughing and the loud-laughing is a wall the cold cannot cross.*

*This is the Mump-work. They think it is foolishness. I accept this. The duty does not need to be understood. It needs to be done.*

*The father chases. The mother holds her drink-water high. The bright-child laughs and her fire blazes and the moths fly up in a great golden storm and the cold-smell flinches.*

*It thins. It pulls back into the floor-crack beneath the stick-no-one-owns. Gone. For now.*

*I drop the foot-thing. I sit. I pant. I wag.*

*The bright-child is looking at me. She sees more than the others. Her eyes are asking a question her mouth does not know how to make yet.*

*One day she will know the question. One day she will know the answer.*

*But not today. Today she is seven and three-quarters, and the house is warm, and the corners are clear.*

*I put my chin on my paws.*

*Good.*
