Clementine's best friend was called Eliza Huang, and she was the second-bravest person Clementine knew, after herself.

Eliza was brave in a different way than Clementine. Clementine was brave about dark hallways and strange sounds and the kind of things that required you to go toward the thing you were frightened of instead of away from it. Eliza was brave about people. She would say things to grown-ups that made other children's eyes go wide. She had once told Mrs. Calloway, the headmistress, that the school's policy on packed lunches was "a bit authoritarian, actually," and had not even blinked when Mrs. Calloway's eyebrows had gone up so far they nearly left her face.

They sat together at lunch, the way they always did, at the table by the window where you could see the playing field and the old oak tree and, if you looked far enough, the edge of the hills where the village ended and the valley began.

"My dad says we might get a dog," said Eliza, biting into an apple with the decisive crunch of someone who considers fruit a task to be completed. "A cockapoo. What even is a cockapoo? It sounds made up."

"It's a real thing," said Clementine. "It's half cocker spaniel, half poodle."

"That's exactly what a made-up dog would be," said Eliza. "Two real dogs in a trench coat pretending to be a third dog. What's Beau?"

"He's just… Beau," said Clementine. And she meant it in a way that was much larger than Eliza could have known.

"But what breed?"

"He’s maybe Golden Retriever and something else. I don’t know what."

"Something else," said Eliza. "Very helpful. Very scientific."

Clementine smiled. She wanted to say: *The something else is the part that matters.* But she did not say it because she did not know how to say it without sounding strange, and Clementine had learned, in the way that children learn these things, that there were some true things that made you sound strange and some true things that did not, and the trick was knowing which was which before you opened your mouth.

"He does this funny thing," she said instead. "We call it Mumpkins. He goes completely mad… steals shoes, crashes into things, runs around like he's lost his mind."

Eliza laughed. "Our cat does that. The zoomies. It's because they have too much energy."

"No," said Clementine. "It's not the zoomies."

She said it too quickly. Too seriously. Eliza looked at her with the expression she wore when she was about to ask a question that would require a real answer.

"What is it then?"

Clementine opened her mouth. She was going to say something. She could feel the words forming… words about the gold-light hour and the corners that felt thin and the way Beau looked at places where there was nothing to see and how the house felt different after he had finished his performance. Real words. True words. Words that described the shape of the thing she had been building in her mind.

But the words did not come out.

Instead, what came out was: "I don't know. He's just weird sometimes. It's funny."

Eliza nodded and bit her apple and said, "Dogs are weird," and the moment closed like a book, and the real words went back to the place inside Clementine where she kept the things she could not say.

It was not that Eliza would have laughed at her. Eliza was not a laugher-at. It was not that Eliza would have told anyone. Eliza was a vault.

It was that the words, once spoken aloud in this room with its strip lighting and its smell of fish fingers and its sensible, explainable everything, would become smaller. They would become a funny story about a silly dog. And they were not a funny story about a silly dog. They were something else. Something that needed the gold-light hour and the quiet of the house and Beau's brown eyes to be true. Something that would shrink if she pulled it out into the fluorescent light of the school dining hall and held it up for someone else to look at.

So she kept it.

She was getting quite good at keeping things.

After lunch, they went outside. The playing field was wet and the sky was white and the old oak at the edge of the grounds stood bare and patient, waiting for spring the way old things wait for things… without hurrying, without worrying, knowing it would come because it had always come.

Clementine looked at the oak and thought of Beau.

Not because Beau looked like a tree. He did not look like a tree. He looked like a dog who had been assembled from spare parts by someone who was very talented but slightly disorganised. But because the oak had the same quality Beau had when the Grandmother said his name. The quality of being rooted in something deeper than what you could see. Of being more than its visible shape. Of standing in one place and meaning it.

"Clem?" said Eliza. "You're doing that thing where you stare at something and go quiet."

"Sorry," said Clementine. "I was thinking."

"About what?"

"About how some things are more than what they look like."

Eliza considered this. She was good at considering things. "Like Mr. Patterson?" she said. "He looks like a supply teacher but he's actually quite good at maths."

Clementine laughed. A real laugh. The kind of laugh that is a door swinging open between two people, letting the warmth through.

"Yes," she said. "Exactly like Mr. Patterson."

It was not exactly like Mr. Patterson. But it was close enough, and close enough was all she had right now, and Eliza's company was warm and solid and real in a way that mattered even if Eliza could not see the things Clementine was learning to see.

They walked around the field twice and talked about ordinary things… a programme they both watched, a book Eliza was reading, whether Oliver Marsh had actually put a second raisin up his nose or whether that was just a rumour… and Clementine felt the two parts of her life sitting side by side inside her, not touching, not fighting, just there. The ordinary part and the other part. The part that could be explained and the part that could not.

She did not know yet that this was what growing up felt like. The slow discovery that you contain more rooms than you thought, and some of the rooms do not have doors that open onto each other, and you have to learn to live in all of them at once.

She was seven and three-quarters. She was learning.

On the walk home from school, she passed the Grandmother's cottage. The blue door was closed. The garden was doing its quiet, complicated growing. And in the window, just for a moment, she saw the Grandmother looking out, and the Grandmother saw her, and the Grandmother smiled and raised one hand.

Not a wave. A salute. The kind of gesture one person makes to another when they share a secret that neither of them has spoken aloud.

Clementine raised her hand back.

Then she walked home, and Beau was waiting at the door, and his tail was wagging, and the house was warm, and the corners were clear.
