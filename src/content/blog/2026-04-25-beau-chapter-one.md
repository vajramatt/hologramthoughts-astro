Beau came to live with the Finch family on a Tuesday in October, when the leaves were turning and the wind smelled like rain and woodsmoke.

He was not a puppy. He was not old. He was somewhere in between… the kind of dog who had already decided what he thought about most things and was not interested in changing his mind.

He had brown eyes and a golden coat and one ear that stood up and one ear that did not, as though his ears had discussed the matter and agreed to disagree.

Clementine Finch, who was seven and three-quarters and took the three-quarters very seriously, sat on the kitchen floor and looked at him. He sat on the kitchen floor and looked at her.

“He’s perfect,” she said.

“He smells like a barn,” said her father.

“He smells like adventure,” said Clementine, which was the same thing, really, depending on your point of view.

Her mother knelt down and held out her hand. Beau sniffed it once, carefully, as though checking her credentials. Then he licked her fingers and wagged his tail and that was that. He was home.

In the first week, the Finch family learned three things about Beau.

The first thing was that he was, most of the time, an extraordinarily good dog. He sat when you asked. He came when you called. He slept at the foot of Clementine’s bed with his chin on his paws and sighed the kind of deep, contented sighs that made the whole room feel safer. On these days, Clementine’s mother would scratch behind his good ear and say, “What a lovely Mr. Tumpkins you are today.”

The name stuck. When Beau was good… and he was usually good… he was Mr. Tumpkins.

The second thing they learned was that sometimes, without any warning at all, Beau would go absolutely crackers.

He would steal a shoe and parade through the house with it. He would skid across the kitchen floor and crash into the bin. He would take a sock from the laundry basket and fling it in the air and catch it and fling it again, and when you tried to take it from him he would dodge you with a look of pure wicked delight, as if this were the finest game ever invented and you were lucky to be included.

On these days, Clementine’s father would fold his arms and say, “He’s being Mr. Mumpkins again.”

The name stuck too. When Beau was silly, he was Mr. Mumpkins.

The third thing they learned took a little longer.

Because sometimes… not often, but sometimes… Beau would do something truly dreadful.

He would dig a hole in the garden so deep you could lose a boot in it. He would bark at the empty corner of the sitting room at three in the morning until the whole house was awake. He would tear a cushion apart as though it had personally offended him and scatter the stuffing across the floor like snow.

On these nights, Clementine’s mother would stand in the doorway in her dressing gown and say, very quietly, “Rumpkins. You dreadful little turd.”

She always felt bad about it afterward. She would make him a bit of toast to apologize, and Beau would eat it with dignity, as though accepting a formal apology from a foreign government.

But Clementine noticed something.

She did not mention it right away, because she was seven and three-quarters and had learned that grown-ups did not always want to hear the things she noticed. But she noticed it all the same.

She noticed that Beau was never Mumpkins for no reason.

It always happened at the same time… late afternoon, when the light was going gold and strange, or just after dark, when the house was settling into its nighttime sounds. He would be lying peacefully by the fire, chin on paws, full Tumpkins, and then his head would come up. His ears… even the floppy one… would go forward. His nose would twitch.

And then the performance would begin. The shoe. The sock. The skidding. The crashing.

And while everyone was looking at Beau, Clementine would look at what Beau had been looking at.

The corner. The hallway. The space under the stairs.

There was never anything there.

But sometimes, just sometimes, the air in those places felt different. Not cold exactly. Not warm. Just… thin. Like the space between two pages of a book. Like the house had taken a breath and not quite finished letting it out.

She did not have a word for it.

But Beau did.

Beau’s word was a low, rumbling growl that started in his chest and never quite reached his throat. A sound he made only when he thought no one was listening.

Clementine was always listening.
