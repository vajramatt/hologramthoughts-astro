The next morning, the Grandmother came back.

This was unusual. The Grandmother came on Fridays. She did not come on Saturdays. Saturday was her garden day, the day she waged her slow, patient war against the bindweed and the slugs and the particular rosebush that had been refusing to flower for three years out of what the Grandmother described as “pure spite.”

But here she was, at half past nine, coming through the back door with the same tin, which now contained scones instead of flat cake, and the same expression she always wore, which was the expression of a woman who has decided something and is not interested in being talked out of it.

“Mum?” said Clementine’s mother, halfway through her second cup of tea and not yet prepared for visitors. “Is everything all right?”

“Everything’s fine. I’ve brought scones. I thought Clementine and I might go for a walk.”

“It’s freezing.”

“Walks are for freezing days. That’s what coats are for. Clementine?”

Clementine was sitting at the table with a bowl of cereal she had stopped eating three minutes ago because Beau was under the table and Beau’s opinion about cereal was that it should be shared, and his method of expressing this opinion involved resting his chin on her knee and looking up at her with an expression of such profound and dignified longing that refusing him felt like a cruelty.

“Yes please,” said Clementine.

“Beau can come,” said the Grandmother. And the way she said his name — the careful way, the True-name way — made Clementine’s heart do something quick and complicated that she did not have time to examine because the Grandmother was already pulling on her gloves and looking at her with the particular impatience of a woman who has never understood why other people take so long to put on shoes.

They walked. Not Clementine’s Saturday route — not down the lane past the goat or through Cotter’s Field. The Grandmother’s route. Along the high path above the village, where the hedgerows were bare and the fields fell away to the left and you could see the whole valley, grey and silver in the November light, with the river at the bottom like a vein of dark water and the church spire rising from the village like a finger pointing at nothing in particular.

Beau walked between them. Not ahead, not behind. Between. His shoulder touching the Grandmother’s skirt on one side and Clementine’s coat on the other, as though he were holding them together, or as though they were holding him.

The Grandmother did not speak for a long time. Clementine knew better than to rush her. The Grandmother’s silences were not empty. They were full of the thing she was about to say, the way a glass is full before you pour it.

They reached the bench at the top of the path — the iron bench with the small brass plaque that said *For Margaret, who loved this view*, and which the Grandmother always touched with her fingertips as she sat down, though she had never said who Margaret was and Clementine had never asked because some questions answer themselves if you leave them alone long enough.

The Grandmother sat. Clementine sat. Beau sat at the Grandmother’s feet and looked out at the valley with the expression of a creature who has seen this view before, many times, in many lives, and has never grown tired of it.

“I saw,” said the Grandmother. “Last night. I saw what happened in the sitting room.”

Clementine’s chest went tight. “You saw the—”

“I saw you see it. That’s what I saw. And I saw Beau.” She paused. “The whole of him.”

The valley was quiet. A crow called from somewhere in the bare trees below. The wind moved through the hedgerow with a sound like someone turning pages.

“I was going to wait,” said the Grandmother. “I was going to let you come to it on your own, because that’s how it should be done. You shouldn’t be told these things. You should find them. But then I thought… I found mine alone, and it was very lonely, and I don’t want that for you.”

She looked at Clementine. Her eyes were clear and sharp and full of something that Clementine recognised as the same thing she had seen in them last night, in the sitting room, when the Grandmother had nodded.

“I’m going to tell you something I’ve never told anyone,” said the Grandmother. “Not your grandfather, God rest him. Not your mother. No one. And I’m telling you because you’re ready, and because you’ve earned it, and because that dog—” she looked down at Beau, “—that dog is not just a dog, and you know it, and I know it, and it’s time one of us said it out loud.”

Beau’s ear twitched. The floppy one. He did not look up. He kept his eyes on the valley.

“I was nineteen,” said the Grandmother. “It was June. I was home from my first year at university and I was full of opinions and certainty and all the things you are full of at nineteen when you think you have understood the world because you have read some books about it.”

She smiled. Not at Clementine. At the memory.

“There was a path I used to walk. Not this one. A path near my parents’ house, up in Northumberland. It ran along the edge of a wood and then through a gap in a stone wall and then across a moor to a little lake that nobody visited because it was too far from anything and the path was bad and the moor was boggy and there was nothing there except the water and the sky and the feeling that you were standing in a place that was older than anything you could name.”

“I’d walked it a hundred times. More. I knew every stone. Every turn. Every boggy bit where you had to step on the tussocks or lose your boot.”

She paused. The wind moved the bare branches above them.

“But this time I got lost.”

She said it simply. The way you say a thing that is simple and enormous at the same time.

“I didn’t take a wrong turn. I didn’t wander off the path. The path moved. Or I moved. Or the light changed and the moor changed with it and suddenly the place I was standing was not the place I had been standing a moment before. The lake was gone. The stone wall was gone. The wood was… further away than it should have been. And the light…”

She stopped. She looked at Clementine.

“You know the light I mean.”

“The gold-light hour,” said Clementine, very quietly.

“Yes,” said the Grandmother. “Except it wasn’t an hour. It was just… there. All around. The way it is when the world goes soft between. And I was in it. Not at the edge of it. In it.”

Beau shifted at her feet. A small movement. A settling. As though he were listening to a story he already knew.

“I wasn’t frightened. Not at first. I was nineteen and I didn’t believe in being frightened of open spaces. But then I tried to walk home and the path wasn’t there. And I tried another direction and it wasn’t there either. And the light didn’t change. It should have been getting darker — it was evening — but the gold light just stayed, and the moor went on in every direction, and I could not find the edge of it.”

She was quiet for a moment.

“Then I was frightened.”

“How long were you lost?” said Clementine.

“I don’t know. That’s the truth of it. I was there for what felt like hours, but when I got home it had been twenty minutes. My mother hadn’t even started to worry. She said, ‘Back already?’ and I stood in the kitchen and I didn’t know how to say that I had been gone for much longer than already. That I had been somewhere else entirely.”

“What happened? How did you get home?”

The Grandmother looked down at Beau. She put her hand on his head. He leaned into her palm.

“Something found me,” she said. “I was standing on the moor and the gold light was everywhere and I couldn’t tell north from south or real from not-real, and then I felt something beside me. Not touching me. Just… there. The way you feel someone standing next to you in a dark room. A presence. A warmth. Something with weight and patience and a smell like… I don’t know how to describe it. Like the air before rain, and fur, and something very old.”

Clementine’s hand found Beau’s ear. The floppy one.

“I couldn’t see it. Not properly. I saw… something. Low to the ground. Moving beside me. A shape that was there when I looked sideways but not when I looked straight at it. The way you see stars better when you don’t look directly at them.”

“And it showed you the way home?”

“It walked. I followed. It didn’t look back. It didn’t wait for me. It just walked, and I walked, and the moor changed around us, and the gold light began to thin, and then there was the stone wall, and there was the gap, and there was the path, and there was the wood, and I was home.”

“And the thing?”

“Gone. I turned around and there was nothing on the moor but the heather and the evening and a feeling like… like the air had just finished breathing out.”

They sat on the bench. The valley was still. The crow had gone quiet. The wind had dropped.

“I went back,” said the Grandmother. “The next day. And the next. And many times after that. I walked that moor for fifty years. I never got lost again. I never saw the gold light like that again. I never felt the presence.”

She looked at Clementine.

“But I never forgot what it felt like to be found.”

The word hung in the air. *Found.* Not rescued. Not saved. Found. The way you find a thing that was always there, waiting to be noticed.

“And when a dog with one floppy ear and brown eyes turned up at your door one evening and sat on the kitchen floor and looked at your mother as though he were checking her credentials,” said the Grandmother, “I knew. Not what he was, exactly. Not in any way I could explain to your mother, who would have looked at me the way she looks at me when I tell her the bindweed has intentions. But I knew he was the same kind of thing. The same… office. The same duty. Standing between the bright ones and the cold things that press at the edges.”

“Bealú,” said Clementine.

The Grandmother went very still.

“What did you say?”

“Bealú. It’s his name. His real name. It means…” Clementine frowned. She did not know how she knew this. She had not read it anywhere. She had not been told. The word had simply been there, inside her, the way a song is inside you before you sing it. “It means the mouth of things. The threshold. The guardian of the doorway.”

The Grandmother’s eyes filled. She did not cry. Clementine had never seen the Grandmother cry and suspected the Grandmother would consider it an inefficient use of time. But her eyes filled and her chin trembled once and she took a breath and she said, “Yes. That’s it. That’s exactly it.”

Beau looked up at Clementine. Then at the Grandmother. Then back at Clementine.

His tail wagged. Once. Slowly. The old wag. The one that was not excitement or greeting but acknowledgement. The wag that said: *Yes. You have it now. The whole of it.*

“He won’t stay,” said the Grandmother.

She said it gently. But she said it.

“I know,” said Clementine. And she did know. She knew it the way she knew his name… whole, not built from parts. The guardian comes when the house needs one. The guardian goes when the house can stand on its own. That is the duty. That is the shape of it.

“But that doesn’t mean the end,” said the Grandmother. “The one on the moor found me once and I have carried it with me for fifty years. It’s in me. The finding is in me. And Beau is in you now. Whatever he is, wherever he goes… he’s in you.”

Clementine leaned against the Grandmother’s shoulder. The Grandmother put her arm around her. Beau pressed his warm weight against both their legs.

They sat on the bench with the brass plaque for Margaret who loved this view, and the valley lay below them, and the village sent up its small smokes, and somewhere in the bare trees the crow called again, and the world was very old and very large and full of things that could not be explained and did not need to be.

After a while, the Grandmother said, “Right. My knees are frozen and those scones won’t eat themselves. Home.”

They walked back down the path. Beau walked between them. The village came up to meet them with its rooftops and its chimney smoke and its church spire and its pub and its post office and all the things that made it a place where people lived ordinary lives and did not generally go in for admitting they were on the edge of something older.

But they were. And now Clementine knew it. And the knowing did not make the ordinary things less real. It made them more.

The scones were excellent.
