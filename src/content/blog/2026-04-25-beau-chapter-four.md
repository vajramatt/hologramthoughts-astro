The Rumpkins episode came on a Sunday night, two weeks after the slipper incident, and it was not funny at all.

It started with the rain.

It had been raining all day… not the cheerful, pattering kind of rain that makes you want to sit by the window with cocoa, but the heavy, grey, relentless kind that makes the house feel smaller and the sky feel closer and the afternoon feel like it will never end.

Clementine had been restless. She had read her whale book twice. She had drawn a picture of Beau that looked more like a horse. She had asked her mother if she could bake something and her mother had said yes and then regretted it almost immediately because Clementine’s idea of baking involved using every bowl in the kitchen and at least one saucepan that had no business being involved.

By evening, the house smelled like slightly burnt scones and the windows were fogged and the rain was still falling and Beau was lying in the hallway with his chin on the floor, very still, watching the front door.

Not the way he usually watched things. Not with the alert, ear-forward attention of the Mumpkins warning. This was different. This was the stillness of a dog who is not resting but waiting.

Clementine’s father stepped over him on the way to the kitchen. “You all right, old man?” he said, and ruffled Beau’s ears, and Beau did not wag.

That was the first wrong thing. Beau always wagged for the father. Always.

The second wrong thing happened at half past ten, when the house was dark and everyone was in bed and the rain had finally stopped, leaving a silence that felt too large for the space it filled.

Beau barked.

Not the bright, sharp bark of a dog who has seen a squirrel or heard the postman. This was a deep, rough, ragged sound that came from somewhere low in his body, and it did not stop.

He was standing in the sitting room, facing the far corner… the corner where the old fireplace met the wall, where the plaster had cracked years ago and been painted over but never properly fixed. He was barking at the crack. At the place where the wall met the floor. At something no one else could see.

Clementine’s mother came downstairs first, dressing gown on, hair wild. “Beau. Beau. *Beau.* It is half past ten on a Sunday. There is nothing there.”

Beau did not stop.

Her father came down next, bumping into the doorframe because he had not put on his glasses. “What’s he going on about?”

“The corner. The same corner. There is *nothing there.*”

Beau stopped barking. And then he did something he had never done before.

He began to dig.

Not in the garden. In the house. At the base of the wall, where the crack ran down to the floor. He dug at the carpet with his front paws, clawing and scraping, pulling the edge of the rug back from the skirting board with a determination that was frantic and focused and completely unlike the cheerful hole-digging he did in the garden.

“Beau! No!” Her mother grabbed his collar. He pulled against her. Not growling, not snapping… Beau would never snap… but pulling with his whole weight, straining toward the crack in the wall as though something behind it required his immediate and desperate attention.

Her father helped pull him away. Together they hauled him to the kitchen and closed the door and stood on the other side of it, listening to him scratch at it twice and then go quiet.

“Rumpkins,” said her mother. But she did not say it the usual way. She said it quietly, and she did not offer to make toast.

They went back to bed. The house settled. The silence came back, and this time it stayed.

But Clementine did not go back to sleep.

She lay in bed and listened. She listened to her parents’ door close. She listened to the boiler tick. She listened to the house doing all the things houses do at night that you only hear when you are listening for something else.

And then she got up and went downstairs and opened the kitchen door.

Beau was lying on the kitchen floor. He looked up at her. In the thin light from the window, she could see that he had been panting. His eyes were tired. Not sleepy-tired. The other kind of tired. The kind her mother got sometimes when she had been worrying about something for a long time and the worrying had used up all her fuel.

Clementine sat down on the kitchen floor in her pyjamas, and Beau put his head in her lap, and she stroked the ear that would not stand, and she said, very quietly, “I know you weren’t being bad.”

She did not know how she knew. She could not have explained it to anyone. But she knew.

She knew the way you know that a storm is coming before the sky changes. The way you know someone is looking at you before you turn around. The way you know, when you are seven and three-quarters, things that grown-ups have forgotten how to know.

Beau breathed out. A long, slow breath. The kind of breath that sounds like putting something heavy down.

And the house felt lighter.

Not warmer. Not safer exactly. Just… lighter. As if something that had been pressing against the walls from the outside had leaned back. Just a little. Just enough.

Clementine stayed on the kitchen floor until her feet were cold and her eyes were heavy. Then she went back to bed, and Beau followed her, and he lay at the foot of her bed with his chin on his paws.

He did not sleep. But he rested.

And in the morning, Clementine’s mother found the corner of the sitting room carpet pulled back and the edge of the rug chewed and the plaster dust from the old crack scattered on the floor, and she sighed and said, “That dreadful little turd,” and made Beau a piece of toast, and Beau ate it with dignity.

But Clementine saw her mother pause, just for a moment, as she looked at the crack in the wall. And she saw her mother frown. And she saw her mother reach out and touch the plaster, lightly, with her fingertips, the way you touch something that is colder than it should be.

And then her mother shook her head and said, “I’ll call someone about that crack,” and the moment was over.

But Clementine kept it. She kept it the way she kept interesting stones and unusual feathers and the small, important things that grown-ups walked past without seeing.

She was building something in her mind. She did not know what it was yet. It did not have a shape or a name. But it had edges, and the edges were getting clearer.

*The rain-night.*

*The cold-smell has been building all day. Growing thicker in the wet. Rain makes the between-places soft… the walls forget their edges when the water comes, and the forgetting lets the cold-smell press closer.*

*I have been watching the floor-crack since morning. The old crack. The one the wall-people painted over but did not fix. You cannot paint over a between-place. You can only stand at it.*

*By the dark-time, the cold-smell is not pressing. It is pushing. I can feel it in my teeth. In the roots of my claws. In the place behind my eyes where the old knowing lives.*

*Something is trying to come through.*

*Not the thin-thing from the coat-corner. That was a watcher. A small wanting. This is different. This is a pusher. It has weight. It has patience. It has been working at this crack for longer than this family has lived in this house.*

*I bark because the barking is a wall too. Not as strong as the loud-laughing but stronger than silence. The mother comes. The father comes. They do not understand. I do not need them to understand. I need them awake. I need the house full of warm-sounds and moving-bodies and the particular hum that a house makes when its people are in it and paying attention. A house full of people is harder to push through than a house full of sleepers.*

*But they take me away. They put me in the food-room and close the door.*

*This is the cost. The Rump-work costs more than the Mump-work. The Mump-work costs a slipper and a scolding. The Rump-work costs trust. They look at me differently after the Rump-work. The mother’s voice goes flat. The father does not ruffle my ears.*

*I accept this. I have accepted it before. In different legs. In different fur. The ones I guarded on the hill did not thank me either. They threw stones. I stayed anyway. The duty does not require gratitude. It requires presence.*

*Then the bright-child comes.*

*She opens the door and sits on the cold floor in her sleeping-clothes and puts her hand on my ear… the one that will not stand, the one that has not stood properly in any of my lives, the old ear, the ear that heard the first cold-smell on the first hill in the first fur… and she says the thing.*

*I know you weren’t being bad.*

*She does not say it in the True-name. She does not say it in any name. She says it in a voice that is just hers, just the bright-child, just the small-fire speaking.*

*And the pushing stops.*

*Not because of me. Because of her. Because the cold-smell felt her fire turn toward the crack, and her fire is young but it is real and the cold-smell is not ready for a fire that knows it is there.*

*The pusher leans back. The crack goes quiet. The walls remember their edges.*

*She goes back to the sleeping-place. I follow. I lie at the foot of her bed.*

*I do not sleep. I watch the corners. I listen to the floor-cracks. I count her breaths.*

*She breathes nine hundred and twelve times before the morning comes.*

*The corners hold.*
